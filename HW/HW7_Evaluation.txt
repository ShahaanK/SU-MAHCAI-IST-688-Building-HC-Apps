3A. ARCHITECTURE

The bot is a chat page with two tools, and the LLM decides which one a message
needs. The heavy work happens once, offline, before the app runs.

Cleaning the CSV
----------------
The file has 1,290 rows about 177 client companies, all published between July
30 and August 6, 2024. It needed three kinds of cleanup.

Duplicates come first. Whole company blocks appear twice (KKR, Goldman Sachs,
Bain Capital, Accenture, Apple, Microsoft and others), and some URLs appear
once with only a description and again with the full article. I kept one
article per client and URL, choosing the longest version, which leaves 1,090
articles. A few URLs are filed under two clients, like a Big Tech roundup
under both Apple and Alphabet. Those stay once per client, since each is news
about a different client.

Some company names carry stray spaces (" TPG ", "Samsung Electronics "), so a
company filter would never match them until they were stripped.

Every Document reads <title> Description: <description>, and about a fifth go
on with content: <full text>. I split those into separate fields.

The database, built outside the app
-----------------------------------
HW7_build_db.py embeds every article with text-embedding-3-small and saves two
committed files: the vectors as a 1,090 x 1,536 NumPy array (6.7 MB) and the
article records as JSON. The page only opens them. Loading them took 0.01
seconds, and the whole page loads in 1.4 seconds in AppTest, which is what the
handout's suggestion to build the DB outside the app was after.

I used NumPy instead of ChromaDB. At 1,090 vectors, exact cosine search is one
matrix multiply, so an index adds nothing. More important, requirements.txt
leaves chromadb unpinned, so a committed Chroma folder written by my local
1.5.9 could fail to open on whatever version Streamlit Cloud installs. A .npy
file has no such risk.

Grouping coverage into stories
------------------------------
Several outlets often cover the same event, and a top-10 list shouldn't show
three copies of it. Within each client, two articles whose embeddings have a
cosine similarity of 0.78 or more count as the same story. I picked 0.78 by
reading pairs in bands. From 0.78 up, they were nearly all different outlets
on one event (Advantest's raised outlook, Amazon's Q2 miss, the Google
antitrust ruling). Below about 0.76 they became separate news that only shared
a topic, like two different Acer laptop deals. The result is 861 stories. 133
of them have more than one article, and the largest has seven. The number of
distinct outlets per story is kept as a coverage signal.

Interest scores, assigned offline
---------------------------------
Retrieval can't answer "find the most interesting news": embedding the word
"interesting" and taking the nearest articles ranks nothing. So the build
script has gpt-5-mini read every article once, 20 per call, with a rubric
written for a law firm watching its clients. For each article it returns four
fields:

- about_client: whether the article is about the client or only shares its
  name.
- score, 0 to 10. Litigation, enforcement, antitrust, large settlements, tax
  disputes, major M&A, mass layoffs, breaches and fatal safety incidents score
  highest. Product reviews and stock-ticker filler score lowest.
- category, a one- or two-word label.
- why, one sentence on why it matters to the firm.

Scoring all 1,090 articles took 1,142 seconds (19 minutes). The scores are
saved, so the ranking costs nothing at runtime and is the same on every run.
That matters because gpt-5 models don't accept a temperature setting, so live
scoring would drift.

Name collisions turned out to be the main data-quality problem for a client-
monitoring bot. 252 of the 1,090 articles were flagged as not about their
client. The most affected names:

Client       Off-topic  Example
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
VINCI        10         Leonardo da Vinci, the Olympics "Last Supper" scene
NEC          9          Birmingham's NEC venue
Oracle       8          a Breville "Oracle" espresso machine
Chubb        7          Browns running back Nick Chubb
Silver Lake  6          the LA neighborhood

Caterpillar's garden-insect Q&A, Eni Aluko, the KKR cricket team, the alphabet
song, the Amazon rainforest and The Points Guy were caught the same way.

The two tools
-------------
rank_news(company?, top_n?) sorts by stored score, then the number of outlets
covering the story, then date. It keeps one article per story and leaves out
anything flagged off-topic. Each ranked story carries its stored category and
"why" sentence, and that is where the context for "interesting" news comes
from.

search_news(query, company?, top_k?) runs a cosine search over the stored
vectors. It drops hits below 0.25 similarity and name collisions, and keeps
one article per story and per URL. The optional company filter accepts aliases
(Meta for Facebook, Google for Alphabet, Foxconn for Hon Hai Precision). When
the company isn't a client, the tool says so and searches all articles for it.

The flow is the same two-call pattern as HW 5. The first call sees the
conversation and both tools and writes the tool arguments. The second call
gets the tool results and no tools, so it can only answer. Its prompt asks for
rankings as a numbered list, each story with a linked headline, date, outlet,
what happened and a "Why it matters" sentence, and for searches to list only
the relevant hits, newest first. It also states the date range and forbids
outside knowledge, so the bot never calls 2024 news "recent". Memory holds the
last 5 interactions.

Provider
--------
The OpenAI account ran out of credits partway through this assignment

3B. HOW I KNOW THE RANKING IS GOOD

A gold set, written before any scoring
--------------------------------------
Before any model had scored anything, I read the CSV and wrote gold_set.json.
It lists 15 must-see stories that a law firm should not miss: Intel's 15,000
layoffs, the Google antitrust ruling, JPMorgan weighing a suit against the
CFPB over Zelle, Japan's corrective order against Toyota, Nvidia's two DOJ
probes, Coca-Cola's $6 billion IRS case, Infosys's $4 billion tax demand,
Amazon suing Nokia, Novartis suing the FDA, Meta's $1.4 billion Texas
settlement, the fatal Tesla FSD crash, the R1 RCM take-private, Apax taking
Thoughtworks private, the EU clearing HPE-Juniper, and Apple pressuring
Tencent over app fees. It also lists 13 noise items, name collisions and
retail filler that must never rank.

I first typed the Tesla headline wrong, so it matched nothing. I fixed the
substring to the real headline without changing which story it names.

Ranking results (gpt-5-mini scores, the app's default)
------------------------------------------------------
Check                         Result
-------------------------------------
Must-see stories in top 10    7 / 15
... in top 15                 10 / 15
... in top 20                 12 / 15
... in top 30                 12 / 15
Noise items that rank at all  0 / 13

All 13 noise items were flagged off-topic, and none of the 252 off-topic
articles can appear in a ranking.

Positions 1 through 7 are Coca-Cola, Intel, Novartis, Tesla, Infosys, Meta and
Google, all must-see stories. Three must-see stories land too low. JPMorgan
vs. the CFPB is #50: it scored 8, while 67 articles scored 9 or 10. The R1 RCM
take-private, also an 8, is #45, and Apple pressuring Tencent is #43.

There are two false positives. A fatal stabbing inside a Walmart store and a
retrospective timeline of the 2019 El Paso Walmart shooting both scored 10/10
and ranked #8 and #11. The cheap model latches onto "fatal incident" language
even when the story has no legal news in it. Infosys also appears twice in the
top 10, because a Reuters report and a Bloomberg opinion column about the same
tax demand fell below the clustering threshold.

Behind most of these is score compression. 26 articles scored 10 and 41 scored
9, so outlet count breaks the ties, and coverage is a weak proxy for legal
importance.

Both models answered only from the tool results; every fact I spot-checked
against the tool output matched. On #1 they kept the tool's order and added a
"Why it matters" line drawn from the stored reason. On #2 both resolved "the
Coca-Cola one" from memory and ran a search filtered to Coca-Cola, returning
the Fortune $6 billion story and the Bloomberg Tax $2.6 billion one. On #4 the
insect article never surfaced. On #5, a re-run of gpt-5-mini after the dedupe
fix returned the same three antitrust stories as gpt-5.5; the Tencent story
had been the 10th hit in the first run and fell out of the top 8. On #6 both
said Boeing is not a client and gave the two Airbus-filed articles that
mention it. On #7, gpt-5-mini read "the most interesting" as singular and
asked the tool for one story, while gpt-5.5 asked for ten.

Question 3 shows one more weakness. Search results are ordered newest first,
so the CFPB story, the most legally important JPMorgan item, came third behind
a branch-expansion story and a rate forecast.

With no OPENROUTER_API_KEY in secrets, the page shows the setup message
instead of a traceback.

Testing turned up two bugs, both fixed before submission:

1. An unfiltered search could return the same URL twice when the feed files it
   under two clients. Search now keeps one article per URL.
2. The local screenshot showed answers garbled into math formatting. Streamlit
   markdown reads a pair of $ signs as LaTeX, and news is full of dollar
   amounts, so the page now escapes $.

I re-ran the antitrust search and took a second screenshot to confirm both
fixes.

4. COMPARING A LOWER-COST AND A HIGHER-COST LLM

The two models are gpt-5-mini ($0.25 per million input tokens, $2 per million
output) and gpt-5.5 ($5 and $30), priced through OpenRouter. That makes
gpt-5.5 20x the input price and 15x the output price. I compared them as the
offline scorer and as the chat model.

As the offline scorer
---------------------
gpt-5.5 rescored a fixed sample of 100 articles with the same prompt
(HW7_evaluate.py compare). The sample was every gold-set article, the two
Walmart crime stories, and a seeded random draw for the rest. I added the
Walmart stories after seeing gpt-5-mini's ranking, and the script labels them
as probes. The run took 65 seconds.

Measure                           Result
--------------------------------------------------
Agree on about_client             94 / 100
Score difference, mean absolute   0.83 points
Scores within 1 point             82 / 100
Spearman rank correlation         0.917
Top-10 overlap within the sample  4 / 10
Mean score (mini vs 5.5)          4.20 vs 3.85
Noise items labeled off-topic     26 / 26 for both

The two scorers mostly agree. The top-10 overlap is low because both put many
articles at 9 or 10, so small shifts reorder the top. Where they disagree,
gpt-5.5 usually has the better judgment. It scored the El Paso timeline 2
against mini's 10, calling it "a retrospective timeline of the 2019 El Paso
Walmart shooting, with limited new legal or business significance". It put the
Walmart stabbing at 8 instead of 10. It gave JPMorgan's CFPB story and the R1
RCM take-private a 9 against mini's 8, and those are the two stories the cheap
ranking buried. It scored an NCR Voyix earnings-date announcement 2 against 5,
noting it has "no results or substantive development yet."

gpt-5.5 was not better everywhere. It scored Apple's pressure on Tencent 6
against mini's 8, and the two split on three articles where the client may
only be named in passing (Bridgepoint, NEA, PAI Partners).

As the chat model
-----------------
Both models answered all eight test messages from the tool results without
inventing facts. gpt-5.5 chose better tool arguments (10 stories instead of 1
for #7) and called out the CFPB story as JPMorgan's most important item.
gpt-5-mini added one off-topic story to its first antitrust answer.

gpt-5.5 was also faster through OpenRouter in this run, which I didn't expect.
Conversation B (6 turns) took 101 seconds on gpt-5-mini and 43 on gpt-5.5, and
conversation A took 59 against 24. Per turn, gpt-5-mini needed 15 to 31
seconds and gpt-5.5 needed 5 to 15.

Summary
-------
The cheap model is good enough to label and score the whole set. It agrees
with the expensive one 94% of the time on relevance, with a rank correlation
of 0.92. Its mistakes cluster at the top of the ranking, which is the part a
law firm reads. For chat, gpt-5.5 gave better-judged answers, and at list
price it should cost only a few cents per question (an estimate; I didn't
meter individual calls).

WOULD I CHANGE ANYTHING?

Yes, the top of the ranking needs a second pass. I tested the simplest version
first: swap in gpt-5.5's scores for the 100 sampled articles and re-rank. That
removed both Walmart crime stories from the top 15 and lifted JPMorgan from
#50 to #16 and R1 RCM from #45 to #14. It also dropped Toyota from #14 to #33,
Nvidia from #12 to #27 and HPE from #20 to #53, stories gpt-5.5 gave a 9 that
then lost the outlet-count tiebreak. Must-see stories in the top 20 went from
12 to 11. A cleaner scorer doesn't help much while so many stories sit at 9
and 10.

The change I'd make is a listwise re-rank: give gpt-5.5 the top 40 candidate
stories in one call and have it order them by legal importance, instead of
scoring each article on its own. That replaces the outlet-count tiebreak with
an actual judgment. I left it out because it is untested, and every result
above describes the app as submitted.

Two smaller changes would also help:

1. Order company searches by importance rather than date, so JPMorgan's CFPB
   story leads.
2. Group same-event articles more aggressively, with a lower threshold or by
   having the LLM group them. That would merge the Infosys opinion column with
   the Reuters report and catch the three Nvidia chip-delay articles that
   stayed separate.

MODEL CHOICE

gpt-5-mini is first in the sidebar and powers the stored ranking for both
models, since only it scored the whole set. On this evidence I'd make gpt-5.5
the chat default: it was more accurate on the follow-ups and faster here, and
the cost gap is small at this volume. The submitted app keeps gpt-5-mini
first.
